Diamond Reviewer Pledge

Give your peer review to the journals that give it back

Diamond Open Access journals are academic journals that are led by scholars. They do not charge for publication nor reading and, unlike commercial journals, they are aligned with scholarly interest. We believe that in a world where much work is published and requires peer review, scholars should prioritize reviewing for such journals as opposed to pay-to-publish or pay-to-read journals.

As signatories of the Diamond Reviewer Pledge, we commit to reviewing at least one article in the next 12 months for a diamond open access journal.

How do I know if a journal is diamond open access?

You can look up journals in the Diamond Discovery Hub, though many new journals have been created and are not listed there yet. Alternatively, check via Open Journals for the ACCESS variable, or visit the journal’s website.

Guidelines for Reviewers

Signing takes a minute; the pledge is about what you do with the next review request that lands in your inbox. These guidelines are suggestions, not conditions of signing.

Before you say yes

  • Check the journal’s model. Look it up in the Diamond Discovery Hub, in Open Journals (the ACCESS variable), in the DOAJ (which records “no APC”), or on the journal’s own “about” and “fees” pages.
  • Give diamond requests priority. Diamond journals are usually run by a handful of volunteers with no editorial office to fall back on; a fast yes is worth much more there than at a journal with a paid managing editor.
  • Budget your reviewing. Decide how many reviews per year you can do well (a common heuristic: about three reviews per paper you publish), and reserve at least one of those slots for a diamond venue.

Be proactive

  • Volunteer. Most diamond journals have a “become a reviewer” form, or you can email the editors with your topics, methods and rough availability. Tick the reviewer-database box when you sign the pledge.
  • Review preprints and community-review submissions. Peer Community In (PCI), diamond overlay journals and community-run review platforms all count.
  • Suggest yourself as a reviewer when colleagues submit to diamond journals, and recommend diamond venues when you are asked where to submit.

Review well

  • Be on time. Agree only to deadlines you can keep, and tell the editor immediately if you slip. Volunteer-run journals cannot absorb silent delays.
  • Review the work, not the person. Be specific, civil and constructive; separate “this is wrong” from “this is not what I would have done”.
  • Prioritise rigour over novelty. Assess the question, design, analysis and the match between claims and evidence. Ask for data, code and materials, and check them where you can.
  • Say what would convince you. Actionable requests are worth more than a verdict.
  • Declare conflicts of interest and use AI tools only within the journal’s policy — never paste an unpublished manuscript into a service that retains it.

Get, and give, credit

  • Consider open review. Sign your report or allow it to be published with the article if the journal offers it; open reports are citable scholarly output.
  • Record your reviews in ORCID (journals can deposit review activity via Crossref) and list them on your CV and in evaluations.
  • Make the norm visible. Add the badge to your website or signature, and mention the pledge when you decline a request from a pay-to-publish or pay-to-read journal — e.g. “I have committed to prioritising peer review for diamond open access journals; I am happy to review for you if the article is openly available without an APC.”
The pledge is additive, not exclusive. Nobody is asking you to boycott anything. Reviewing one article a year for a scholar-led journal is a floor, not a ceiling.

Guidelines for Members of DOA Journals

Reviewers can only prioritise you if they can find you, recognise you as diamond, and trust that their time will be used well. Editors, here is how to make that easy.

Be findable and legible as diamond

  • State it on the landing page: “No article processing charges. No subscription. Authors retain copyright (CC BY).” One sentence, above the fold, not buried in a policies page.
  • Register the facts in DOAJ, the Diamond Discovery Hub, OpenAlex and your indexing services, and keep the APC field explicitly at zero.
  • Publish your scope, criteria and timelines, including median times to first decision and to publication.

Recruit reviewers deliberately

  • Keep a public “review for us” form with expertise keywords, methods and availability, and actually use the resulting database.
  • Invite pledge signatories and say so in the invitation: a line such as “We are a diamond open access journal — free to read, free to publish, authors retain copyright” raises acceptance rates.
  • Recruit early-career researchers, ideally with a co-review or mentored-review scheme that credits both people by name.

Respect reviewer time — it is the journal’s only real budget

  • Desk-screen properly. Never send out a manuscript that is out of scope, incomplete or unreadable.
  • Ask precise questions. A short structured form beats “please comment on the manuscript”.
  • Set realistic deadlines (three to four weeks), send one polite reminder, and never chase a reviewer who has already delivered.
  • Limit rounds. Editors should adjudicate rather than forward every disagreement back to the reviewers.
  • Close the loop. Send the decision letter and the other reports to every reviewer. This is the cheapest form of payment there is.

Credit the work

  • Deposit review activity to ORCID via Crossref, and issue certificates on request.
  • Publish an annual thank-you list of reviewers (with consent).
  • Offer open, DOI-bearing reports for reviewers who want their work to be citable.

Stay diamond, sustainably

  • Use community infrastructure (OJS, Janeway, Episciences, PKP Preservation Network, CLOCKSS) and get DOIs and long-term archiving in place.
  • Fund the journal through institutions, libraries, consortia and grants, not through authors. Publish your funding model.
  • Do not flip to APCs. If costs grow, share them across the community before shifting them onto authors.
  • Adopt transparency policies (data, code and materials availability; preregistration where applicable; Registered Reports) so that reviewers can actually evaluate what they are asked to evaluate.
  • Link to this pledge from your reviewer pages, and tell us about your journal so we can point reviewers to you.

FAQ

Peer review is free labour. Researchers — largely paid from public money — write the papers, review the papers and edit the journals. The publisher coordinates and typesets, and then charges for the result. Estimates of the global value of unpaid reviewing run to well over a billion US dollars per year, and the large commercial publishers report operating margins in the range of 30–40 %, which is money that leaves the science system permanently.

That business model also creates a structural conflict of interest. Under article processing charges, every additional accepted manuscript is additional revenue, so the commercial incentive points towards volume, not towards strict quality control. Reviewers are the brake in that system, and they are the only unpaid part of it. Asking whether the brake should be donated to the party that profits from wearing it out is a fair question.

Meanwhile the people who pay for the research often cannot read it. Much of the literature written by publicly funded researchers, reviewed by publicly funded researchers, sits behind paywalls that neither the general public, nor practitioners, nor colleagues at less wealthy institutions can pass. Where it is open, it is frequently open because someone paid an exorbitant APC: commonly USD 2,000–4,000, and up to roughly EUR 9,500 at the most prestigious titles — figures far above plausible per-article production costs and unaffordable for most of the world’s researchers.

Large publishers have also become data businesses. Elsevier’s parent company RELX derives much of its revenue from analytics and risk products; academic platforms track user behaviour, and researcher and usage data are aggregated into products sold back to universities, funders and, in the case of other RELX units, to third parties including government agencies. See the German Research Foundation’s briefing paper “Data Tracking in Research” (DFG, 2021) and the SPARC work on vendor surveillance. Every review you donate helps sustain that infrastructure.

Finally, “high-ranking” is not a property of the publisher — it is produced by authors, editors and reviewers, and it can be built elsewhere. Diamond journals such as the Journal of Machine Learning Research, Journal of Statistical Software, Glossa, Discrete Analysis, Peer Community Journal and Meta-Psychology demonstrate that fully free-to-read, free-to-publish, rigorously reviewed and highly cited venues are entirely possible. Where you send your review is where you invest your scarce quality-assurance capacity. The pledge asks for one article per year on the scholar-led side of the ledger — and none of it prevents you from also reviewing for the journals in your field you consider important.

A peer-reviewed journal that charges neither readers nor authors, is typically owned and run by the scholarly community rather than a commercial publisher, and normally lets authors retain copyright under an open licence. Diamond overlay journals and community peer review platforms such as Peer Community In fall under the same spirit and count for the pledge.

Waived or discounted APCs do not make a journal diamond — the model, not the individual exemption, is what matters.

The pledge is a good-faith commitment; nobody audits it, and nobody is penalised for a year in which the invitations did not arrive.

Reviewing is rarely evaluated by venue prestige in the first place — it is counted as service. Diamond journals are often better at crediting it: open, citable reports with DOIs, ORCID deposits and public reviewer lists. And because many of these journals are young, reviewers with substantive expertise are frequently invited onto editorial boards, which is service that does appear in evaluations.
